How much structure?

issueopen 4 on record

The corpus's longest-running dispute: predefined paths versus model-directed process; simplicity-first versus structure-first. Positions on record span 2024–2026.

  • responds to Autonomy versus reliabilityThe spectrum stated plainly — more autonomy, more potential value, less reliability; determinism for sensitive or preset workflows.
  • responds to Design loops that prompt agentsSteinberger's loop-era standing reminder — stop prompting agents yourself; design the loops that do — with Osmani's definitional gloss.
  • responds to Find the simplest solution possibleThe standing counterweight to graph maximalism — add complexity only when needed; use agents only where paths can't be hardcoded.
  • responds to Own your control flowFactor 8 — build your own control structures around the loop; good agents are mostly just software.

Is it just fancier retrieval?

issueopen 7 on record

Posed verbatim by the seed thread's OP — the skeptic's question is this issue's sourced statement. The positions below answer it directly; retrieval-failure results and the routing prescription engage it from research and practice.

  • responds to Compiled once, kept currentKarpathy's LLM-wiki principle — knowledge as a persistent, compounding, cross-referenced artifact the model maintains, not something re-derived per query.
  • responds to Legible to humans and machinesThe commenter's opening definition — graphs express complex information in a form both humans and machines can understand, and convert easily to visualization.
  • responds to RAG fails on global questionsThe GraphRAG paper's problem statement — retrieval cannot answer questions about a corpus as a whole, because they are summarization tasks, not retrieval tasks.
  • responds to Route by question typeThe practitioner consensus — vector for lookups, graph for chains; hybrid, not conversion.
  • responds to The graph as abstraction layerThe commenter's constructive move — a graph plus the code that interprets it is a self-defined abstraction layer over the storage format, freeing representation from the format's limits.
  • exemplifies The skeptic's question: just fancier retrieval?The seed thread's OP names the trend's legibility problem and poses the reduction that the whole bundle answers — are graphs merely a fancier retrieval mechanism?
  • responds to Yes and no: storage shapes retrievalThe commenter's direct answer to the reduction question — graphs are about data complexity, and the storage method determines the retrieval method.

Loops versus graphs

issueopen 6 on record

June 2026 named the loop discipline; July named the graph discipline. Positions on record dispute whether the second replaces, contains, or merely renames the first.

  • responds to Graphs contain loopsBouchard's containment claim — the graph is not a replacement for the loop but an extra layer of delegated trust above it.
  • responds to Loop engineering is a simple version of graphsLangChain's formal subsumption — loops are the one-node special case, and production agents need cycles anyway.
  • responds to Loops live inside the nodesThe nesting claim — important nodes still contain loops; the graph organizes, constrains, and connects them. Plus the restraint prescription: graph only the necessary relationships.
  • responds to Stack and extend loopsLoop engineering defined as loop composition — agent, verification, event-driven, and hill-climbing loops stacked into systems.
  • responds to Three tiers of reliabilityThe cleanest layering claim in the corpus — prompt engineering makes a call reliable, loop engineering an agent, graph engineering a group of agents.
  • responds to Workflows vs agentsThe December 2024 distinction both 2026 camps descend from — predefined code paths versus LLMs directing their own process, in a loop.

What is “graph engineering”?

issueopen 8 on record

Within 48 hours of the July 2026 naming event the term carried three competing meanings (twelve words). The definitional positions on record differ on what nodes are (knowledge vs. capability), who designs the paths, and where the knowledge lives.

  • responds to A graph is two things: nodes and edgesThe field guide's buzzword-free minimal definition, cast in knowledge terms — the things you know about, and the connections between them.
  • responds to A map of how something worksThe commenter's closing distillation — the graph is a map you create of how something works, then read back.
  • responds to An untyped edge is one bitThe typed-edge distinction — "related" carries one bit of information; supersedes / depends_on / decided_by / caused carry the meaning.
  • responds to Boring nodes, typed edges, checkpointed stateSimmons' three commitments — the definitional core of graph engineering in its orchestration sense.
  • responds to Explicit graphs an agent can traverseFlowtivity's definition uniting both senses — entities, decisions, and concepts as nodes; typed edges; traversal by the agent — plus the concurrency argument against loops.
  • responds to The AI moves within a pre-designed mapGao Dalie's determinist definition — humans design the objectives, criteria, and the entire path; the AI does not wander.
  • responds to The edge type IS the knowledgeThe maximal typed-edge thesis — relatedness is trivial to detect; the typed edge is what answers "why did this change?"
  • responds to This week's name for an orchestrated system of loopsBouchard's deflationary definition — graph engineering as the newest label on a rename treadmill, for a practice that already existed.
