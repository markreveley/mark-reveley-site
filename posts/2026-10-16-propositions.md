This post explores the concept of a proposition, and the idea of AI/human engineering centaurs.

Let's start by proposing a working definition of a proposition:

> A proposition is the meaning expressed by a statement, interpreted in its context, that can be
evaluated for truth or falsity. Quotations and paraphrases are
representations of that meaning.

This was coined by an LLM in a discussion I am engaged in regarding expressing the essence of atomic claims.

Here is an example of an atomic claim taken by Sean Goedecke's article [Human-AI partnerships are for alignment, not capability](https://www.seangoedecke.com/human-ai-partnerships-are-for-alignment-not-capability/):
> Lots of people think that we’re currently in a world of software engineering centaurs.

My informal reading of the proposition in this statement would be as:

> we're currently in a world of software engineering centaurs.

ie. that which follows "that". 

"Lot's of people think" would be the qualifier, or per the working definiton above, the "context" that the proposition appears in, on a local sentence-specific level. The greater argument would be the "meta-context". In between that could be any number of layers of context abstractions.

In this case, the "meta-context" is easy to fetch: 

> Human-AI partnerships are for alignment, not capability. 

This statement is both the URL and the title, and the essay is structured around it.

The context within which our claim is being made, is in fact a claim itself.

To understand the nature of our claim, regarding engineering centaurs, we need to define engineering centaurs, according to the intention of the source's author. To paraphrase from the article:

> <Centaurs are> human-AI partnerships that are stronger than unassisted AIs or humans

To which we could add "according to some objective measurement."

So, now we have:

1 - a working definition of a proposition
2 - an example claim that can be associated with a proposition
3 - a proposed proposition bound to the example claim
4 - a definition of dependent terms (ie. centaur) necessary to understand to make the claim atomic
5 - an understanding of the context the claim appears within

This raises a question - does the proposition exist within the same context the claim does? According to the working definition, yes, but I propose a question that has not been explicitly decided and whose answer has been implicitly smuggled into the definition:

If a proposition is a verbatim fragment, ie. if 'proposition' defines the part of the sentence following "that" which reads "we're currently in a world of software engineering centaurs", then a proposition is a semantic marker, and simply points to a portion of a claim. In this case, yes, the proposition is to be seen in the context of the claim.

If a proposition is a concept that exists separately from the portion of the claim itself, then no, the proposition is NOT to be seen in the context of the claim. It is to be seen as something that exists wholly outside of the claim, and that the source claim points to.

So, either the idea of a proposition is a pointer to a type of sentence fragment, and nothing more (type 1), or a proposition is a thing that can be pointed to by the portion of the claim associated with the proposition, and nothing more (type 2.)

I am going to make a temporary and completely instinctual decision that, for the purposes of this post, a proposition is a thing that can be pointed to by a portion of the claim associated with the proposition (type 2.)

Now lets revisit our original LLM-coined definition:

> A proposition is the meaning expressed by a statement, interpreted in its context, that can be
evaluated for truth or falsity. Quotations and paraphrases are
representations of that meaning.

This seems to simultaneously support and undermine either type.

"The meaning expressed by a statement" separates clearly the meaning from the statement. So does "quotations and paraphrases are representations of that meaning". This is unambiguous.

"interepreted in its context" violates the rule proposed above, which says a type 2 proposition is NOT to be seen in the context of the claim.

Something needs to give - either our working definition of a proposition needs to be modified, or the claims of the rule above needs to be modified. They cannot co-exist.

I am going to make an argument against the working definition, based on language within the definition itself. If 'meaning' is expresssable as a thing outside of the 'statement', but is pointed to directly by that statement, 'meaning' is individuated conceptually while being dependently associated. If it is individuated conceptually, it must have its own ability to have independent relationships. If it can have its own independent relationships, other claim statements should be able to point to it. If other claim statements can point to it, it must exist independent of the context of all dependent claim statements, unless the contexts are discretely attached, if there is even a method to do so.

Coming back to our example:

The full claim statement
> Lots of people think that we’re currently in a world of software engineering centaurs.

The context
> Lots of people think

The claim statement
> we’re currently in a world of software engineering centaurs.

The proposition
> we’re currently in a world of software engineering centaurs.

At this point, the proposition, although seperata from claim statement 1, is reflected verbatim, in there is no rule that would say otherwise.

Now imagine a new claim statement:
> software engineering centaurs outperform AI engineers and human engineers according to the Centaur benchmark

This new claim statement can point to the same proposition, which is now a shared object, IF a judgement phase has allowed for either a human, agent, or centaur to determine that the semantic meaning of each phrase is close enough to imply shared ownership of the proposition. Let's assume that process has occurred, and shared ownership has been agreed upon.

At this point, the proposition can either:

1 - retain its original wording
2 - be rewritten to better reflect the shared ownership

Option 1 would use sequence as a first class citizen in determining the ability to define. This is arbitrary.

Option 2 would better reflect the shared ownership model, but require a process of rewriting and then judging the change. Let's assume that, like the judgement phase above, this would be made explicit.

I favor option 2, so am going to take the liberty of choosing it for our system.

Here is, thanks to me, but could be thanks to agents or centaurs as well, our new modified proposition:
> Software centaurs exist in the world as of 10-15-2026.

In finding the claim overlap between the statements, we do not find room to express the claim statement 2 makes, that centaurs outperform AI and human engineers. The best we can express is that software centaurs exist in a point in time, which is not that far from a definition. 

The claim that centaurs outperform AI and human engineers needs to be a new proposition:
> Centaurs outperform AI and human engineers as of 10-15-2026

It appears that propositions arise emengently as they are extracted from statements, as new connections between statements and the propositions they link to makes clear the need for the semantic evolution of propositions. Propositions are living approximations across sources. And since propositions are, according to our definition, meaning, this means that meaning is an approximation.

We now have a chance to revisit our working definiton of a proposition, which was:
> A proposition is the meaning expressed by a statement, interpreted in its context, that can be
evaluated for truth or falsity. Quotations and paraphrases are
representations of that meaning.

But could now arguably be expressed as:
> The meaning expressed by a statement, understandable separate from the statement's context, that can be
evaluated for truth or falsity. Quotations and paraphrases are
representations of that meaning.

That was a lot of work to change one part of a line of a definition of a thing that we should like to hope is already defined enough to be used colloquially between humans and agents in conversation without explicit qualification.

"We should like to hope" is a graveyard of dreams.
